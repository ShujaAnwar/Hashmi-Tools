Calorie Calculator — TDEE, BMR, and How Many Calories You Actually Need

Every function your body performs — from breathing and pumping blood to walking, thinking, and digesting food — requires energy. That energy comes from the food you eat, measured in kilocalories (kcal), commonly called "calories." Understanding how many calories your body needs is the foundation of any serious nutrition plan, whether your goal is weight loss, muscle gain, athletic performance, or simply maintaining stable health.

Our Calorie Calculator computes two key numbers: your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) — the calories you burn at complete rest — and your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) — the calories you actually burn in a real day including physical activity. From TDEE, we derive specific targets for weight loss, maintenance, and weight gain.

The Mifflin-St Jeor Formula (Current Gold Standard)

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation, validated in 1990 and recommended by the American Dietetic Association, is the most accurate BMR formula for most adults:

Men: BMR = (10 × weight kg) + (6.25 × height cm) − (5 × age) + 5

Women: BMR = (10 × weight kg) + (6.25 × height cm) − (5 × age) − 161

Worked Example — Female, 28 years, 62 kg, 165 cm:
BMR = (10 × 62) + (6.25 × 165) − (5 × 28) − 161
BMR = 620 + 1,031.25 − 140 − 161 = 1,350 kcal/day

Activity Multipliers: From BMR to TDEE

Your BMR is then multiplied by an activity factor to get TDEE:

Activity Level Description Multiplier
SedentaryDesk job, no exerciseBMR × 1.2
Lightly activeLight exercise 1–3 days/weekBMR × 1.375
Moderately activeModerate exercise 3–5 days/weekBMR × 1.55
Very activeHard exercise 6–7 days/weekBMR × 1.725
Extra activePhysical job + daily trainingBMR × 1.9

Continuing the example: Moderately active → TDEE = 1,350 × 1.55 = 2,093 kcal/day

Setting Calorie Targets by Goal

  • Weight loss (0.5 kg/week): TDEE − 500 kcal/day (1 kg of fat ≈ 7,700 kcal; 500 kcal/day deficit = 3,500 kcal/week ≈ 0.5 kg/week)
  • Weight loss (1 kg/week): TDEE − 1,000 kcal/day (aggressive; not recommended below 1,200 kcal/day for women or 1,500 for men)
  • Maintenance: TDEE ± 100 kcal/day
  • Muscle gain (lean bulk): TDEE + 200–300 kcal/day with high protein (1.6–2.2 g/kg body weight)
  • Fast bulk: TDEE + 500 kcal/day (expect some fat gain alongside muscle)

Calorie Counting Accuracy: Managing Expectations

Calorie calculators provide estimates, not exact measurements. Several sources of real-world variance exist:

  • Formula accuracy: Mifflin-St Jeor predicts BMR within ±10% for about 82% of people — meaning your actual BMR could be 135 kcal higher or lower than calculated
  • Food label errors: US FDA allows a ±20% margin of error on nutrition labels; packaged foods can have significantly more or fewer calories than stated
  • Cooking and processing: Cooking increases caloric availability (cooking gelatinises starch and denatures protein, making more energy extractable)
  • Thermogenic effect of food (TEF): Your body burns 5–30% of a food's calories digesting it — protein has the highest TEF at ~25%, fat the lowest at ~3%

Use your TDEE as a starting point, track actual results for 2–3 weeks, and adjust by 100–200 kcal based on real body weight changes. The calculation gives you the theory; your body gives you the data.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Harris-Benedict or Mifflin-St Jeor formula more accurate?

Mifflin-St Jeor (1990) is consistently more accurate than the original Harris-Benedict (1919) in validation studies. The revised Harris-Benedict (1984) is close in accuracy to Mifflin-St Jeor. This calculator uses Mifflin-St Jeor as it is the current clinical recommendation of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics.

How many calories should I eat to lose weight without losing muscle?

A deficit of 300–500 kcal/day combined with high protein intake (1.6–2.2 g per kg of body weight) and resistance training is the most evidence-supported approach for losing fat while preserving muscle. Larger deficits accelerate muscle loss and slow metabolic rate — the opposite of what most people want.

Why am I not losing weight even at a calorie deficit?

Common reasons include: (1) underestimating calories consumed — food tracking research shows people underreport by 20–40%; (2) overestimating activity level — select "sedentary" if you only exercise 3×/week, not "moderately active"; (3) water retention masking fat loss, especially early in a diet; (4) metabolic adaptation — prolonged deficits reduce TDEE by 10–15% via adaptive thermogenesis.

What is the minimum safe calorie intake?

Most clinical guidelines recommend a minimum of 1,200 kcal/day for women and 1,500 kcal/day for men to ensure adequate micronutrient intake. Very low calorie diets (VLCD) below 800 kcal/day exist but should only be undertaken under medical supervision as they carry risks of electrolyte imbalances, gallstones, and lean mass loss.

Should I eat back calories burned during exercise?

This depends on how you set your TDEE. If you included exercise in your activity multiplier, your TDEE already accounts for those calories — do not eat them back. If you used a sedentary multiplier and track exercise separately, add back 50–75% of estimated exercise calories (not 100%, as fitness trackers and machines overestimate burn by 20–90%).
